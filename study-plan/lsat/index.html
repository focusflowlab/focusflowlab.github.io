How the plan is put together

The LSAT has four multiple-choice sections: two scored Logical Reasoning, one scored Reading Comprehension and one unscored section. There’s also a separate, unscored Argumentative Writing sample that schools see. Since 2024 there are no logic games.

  1. Learn the question types, a bit under half. One or two types a day, untimed first, then timed, plus daily reading passages.
  2. Timed practice, the rest. A full practice test every other week, timed sections in between, and blind review of every hard question.
  3. Final week. Your last practice test about five days out, light review, and a day off before the test.

Logical reasoning gets about two thirds of the time because it’s two of the three scored sections. Any area you tick as a worry gets 50% more.

Use the official practice tests on LawHub, and check current rules at lsac.org.

What makes a plan stick

A plan that survives week two beats a perfect one that doesn’t.

  • Study at the same time each day. Your phone goes in another room for that block.
  • Keep a log of every miss and why. Re-read it before each study day.
  • When you miss a day, don’t double up. Carry on from where the plan says you are.

Questions people ask

How long should I study for the LSAT?

Many people study for 2 to 4 months, often around 120 to 300 hours, depending on their starting score and target.

What is blind review?

After a timed section, redo the questions you weren’t sure of without a timer and before seeing answers. It shows whether misses come from reasoning or from time pressure.

Is this an official LSAT plan?

No. It’s a free planning tool from FocusFlow Lab, and it isn’t connected to LSAC.
