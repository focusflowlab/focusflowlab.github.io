Memory

Why highlighting fails, and how to take notes you remember

Highlighting feels like work. It mostly isn’t. Here’s what to do instead.

By FocusFlow Lab. Published 26 October 2026.

On this page

  1. The problem with highlighting
  2. What works better: active recall
  3. Turn your notes into questions
  4. Keep it short
  5. Space the reviews out
  6. Watch the video

The problem with highlighting

In a well-known review of ten common study methods, John Dunlosky and colleagues rated highlighting and re-reading as low utility: they don’t do much for long-term memory (Dunlosky et al., 2013). Highlighting feels productive because the page looks worked on, but your brain only had to decide what looked important, not remember it.

What works better: active recall

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory instead of reading it again. Each time you recall something, it gets easier to recall the next time. The same review rated practice testing as one of the two most useful methods.

Turn your notes into questions

  1. Split your page: a narrow column on the left, notes on the right.
  2. After a lecture or chapter, write a question in the left column for each main point. “What are the three causes of…?”, not “Causes”.
  3. To review, cover the right side and answer each question out loud or on paper.
  4. Check, and mark the ones you missed. Those are your next review.

Keep it short

You don’t need beautiful notes. A question you can answer in ten seconds is better than a paragraph you’ll never read again. If you’re copying a sentence word for word, stop and ask what question it answers.

Space the reviews out

Answering the questions once helps. Answering them again a day later, then a few days later, helps much more. The spaced review calendar gives you the dates and adds them to your calendar.
